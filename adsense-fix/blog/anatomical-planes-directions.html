Anatomical Planes and Directional Terms

Anatomy Foundations · Updated November 2026
Sagittal Coronal Transverse Directional terms

Before studying any organ, medical students learn the "universal coordinate system" of the human body. Anatomical position and directional terms allow doctors, scientists, and students to describe locations precisely and unambiguously. This guide covers anatomical position, the three major body planes, and the key directional pairs used in anatomy.

1. Standard Anatomical Position

All descriptions assume the body is in standard anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, feet flat and slightly apart, arms at the sides with palms facing forward, and thumbs pointing outward. Directional terms always refer to this position, regardless of how the person is actually positioned.

2. The Three Major Body Planes

PlaneDescriptionDivides body into
Sagittal planeA vertical plane running from front to backLeft and right sections
Midsagittal (median) planeA sagittal plane exactly down the middleEqual left and right halves
Coronal (frontal) planeA vertical plane running from side to sideFront (anterior) and back (posterior) sections
Transverse (horizontal/axial) planeA horizontal plane running parallel to the groundTop (superior) and bottom (inferior) sections
Quick reference:
Sagittal = left/right · Coronal = front/back · Transverse = top/bottom

3. Directional Terms (Opposite Pairs)

TermMeaningOpposite
Anterior (ventral)Toward the front of the bodyPosterior (dorsal)
Posterior (dorsal)Toward the back of the bodyAnterior (ventral)
Superior (cranial)Toward the head / upper partInferior (caudal)
Inferior (caudal)Away from the head / lower partSuperior (cranial)
MedialToward the midline of the bodyLateral
LateralAway from the midline / toward the sideMedial
ProximalCloser to the point of attachment (limb)Distal
DistalFarther from the point of attachment (limb)Proximal
SuperficialClose to the body surfaceDeep
DeepAway from the body surfaceSuperficial

4. Why It Matters

Using these standardized terms prevents dangerous misunderstandings. For example, saying "the injury is on the left side of the chest" is ambiguous without anatomical position, but "the injury is on the anterior-lateral aspect of the right thoracic wall" is precise and actionable in clinical settings.
