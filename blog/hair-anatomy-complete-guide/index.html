Hair Anatomy: The Shaft, Follicle, Root and Growth Cycle

Explore the cuticle, cortex and medulla of a hair shaft, then follow the follicle, bulb, matrix, dermal papilla and growth cycle beneath the skin.

Corpus editorial reference · Updated 2026-11-04

Key takeaways

  • The visible hair shaft is made of dead, keratinized cells; the living growth region is deeper in the follicle.
  • Cuticle, cortex and medulla describe shaft layers; a medulla is not present in every hair.
  • The follicle, hair matrix, dermal papilla, sebaceous gland and arrector pili muscle have distinct roles.

The shaft has layers, but no living core

The hair shaft is the portion that extends above the skin surface. It consists mainly of hard keratinized cells that have completed their growth and lost their nuclei. From the outside inward, the shaft is commonly described as cuticle, cortex, and—when present—medulla. A strand is not a hollow tube filled with a soft inner substance; its central appearance depends on the structure and pigmentation of the keratinized cells.

The cuticle is a thin outer layer of overlapping cells that protects the shaft. The cortex forms most of the shaft’s bulk and contains densely packed keratin and pigment granules. Melanin, produced by pigment cells in the growing follicle, contributes to hair colour. The medulla is the central region and can be discontinuous or absent, especially in some fine hairs. These layers are microscopic, so a magnified diagram is a model rather than a description visible to the naked eye.

The follicle surrounds the root and growing bulb

A hair follicle is an epithelial tube that extends from the epidermis into the dermis. The hair root lies below the skin surface and is held within this follicular structure. At its deepest end, the root expands into a bulb. The bulb contains a matrix of dividing cells that produce the cells that become the hair shaft and parts of the inner root sheath. The growing cells move upward, change their proteins, and become the keratinized hair that emerges from the skin.

At the base of the bulb, the dermal papilla is a connective-tissue projection containing capillaries and signaling cells. It supports the growth region, but the shaft itself is not supplied with blood. The inner root sheath helps shape and guide the developing hair, while the outer root sheath is continuous with the epidermis. Surrounding connective tissue anchors the follicle. A pulled-out shaft may have a club-shaped root end, but that does not mean the entire follicle has been removed.

The hair follicle belongs to a larger skin unit

Many follicles are associated with a sebaceous gland, which releases sebum into the follicular canal. Sebum coats nearby skin and hair; it is not the material from which the shaft is made. A small arrector pili smooth muscle attaches to the follicle and nearby dermis. When it contracts, the hair can become more upright and the skin may form a temporary bump. This reflex is often called goosebumps and is more conspicuous in areas with hair follicles.

Sensory nerve endings around a follicle detect movement of the hair shaft, giving the skin information about light contact. Sweat glands, blood vessels, immune cells, and connective tissue also support the skin environment, though they are not pieces of the hair itself. Distinguishing the hair from its follicle and from neighbouring glands prevents a common mix-up: “what is in a strand?” and “what surrounds a follicle?” are related but different anatomy questions.

  • Sebaceous gland: adds oily sebum to the follicular opening and nearby surface.
  • Arrector pili: a small smooth muscle that changes follicle angle when it contracts.
  • Hair follicle plexus: sensory endings responsive to displacement of the hair.

Hair growth follows a repeating follicle cycle

Follicles cycle through phases rather than producing an indefinitely lengthening shaft at a constant rate. In anagen, matrix cells actively form a growing hair. Catagen is a shorter transition in which growth slows and the lower follicle regresses. In telogen, the follicle is relatively resting; the club hair may remain before shedding and a later growth phase begins. The exact duration and timing vary among follicles and body regions.

A normal cycle includes shedding, and a person has many follicles at different stages at the same time. Hair density, diameter, curl pattern, colour, and length are influenced by genetics, body region, age, hormones, and other factors. A simplified diagram cannot determine why an individual’s hair has changed. New or concerning hair or scalp changes are personal health questions, not conclusions that can be made from a single anatomy illustration.

What an anatomy diagram can and cannot show

An educational cutaway can label the shaft layers, matrix, bulb, papilla, gland, and muscle in one view, but those parts are not all visible together in an ordinary photograph. Their sizes and shapes also differ by follicle type and location. Scalp hair, eyelashes, and fine body hair share broad principles but differ in length, growth timing, and associated structures.

For study, separate three scales: the shaft layers, the follicle that produces the hair, and the skin around the follicle. Then connect the names to function. This avoids treating a strand as a living organ or assuming every hair has an obvious medulla. Questions people ask

Frequently asked questions

What are the three layers of a hair shaft?

The commonly described layers are the outer cuticle, the keratin-rich cortex, and a central medulla when one is present. The medulla can be absent, so not every hair has all three clearly identifiable layers.

Is the visible hair shaft alive?

The exposed shaft is composed mainly of keratinized cells that are no longer living. Hair production occurs lower down in the follicle, where matrix cells divide and become the cells of the growing hair.

What does the dermal papilla do?

The dermal papilla is connective tissue at the base of the hair bulb. Its capillaries and signaling environment support the growing follicle; the hair shaft above the skin is not supplied with blood.

Does every hair have a medulla?

No. A medulla can be absent or discontinuous, depending on the hair. It is safest to describe it as a possible central layer rather than a guaranteed hollow or filled core.

What makes hair stand up during goosebumps?

Contraction of the arrector pili smooth muscle pulls on the follicle and nearby skin. It can make a hair more upright and create a small raised area, although the response is more visible in some regions.
