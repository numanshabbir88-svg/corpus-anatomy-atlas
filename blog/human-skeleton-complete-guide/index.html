Human Skeleton Anatomy: Divisions, Bone Types and Joints

Study the adult skeleton’s axial and appendicular divisions, bone shapes, compact and spongy tissue, major joints, marrow and lifelong remodelling.

Corpus editorial reference · Updated 2026-11-04

Key takeaways

  • The conventional adult count is 206 bones, but age-related fusion and variable sesamoid bones make counts dependent on convention.
  • The axial skeleton forms the body’s central axis; the appendicular skeleton includes limb bones and their girdles.
  • Bone is living tissue that remodels, houses marrow and works with joints, cartilage, ligaments and muscles.

Two divisions organize the adult skeleton

The axial skeleton forms the central axis: skull, auditory ossicles, hyoid bone, vertebral column, ribs, and sternum. It protects the brain, spinal cord, and thoracic organs while providing attachment for muscles of the head, neck, and trunk. The appendicular skeleton consists of the bones of the upper and lower limbs plus the pectoral and pelvic girdles that connect those limbs to the axial framework.

A frequently taught adult total is 206 bones. The number is a convention based on the standard adult pattern: some bones present earlier in life fuse during development, and small sesamoid bones vary between people. Accessory bones and anatomical variation can alter counts. Therefore, “206” is a useful reference count, not a claim that every person has exactly the same set of separate bones at every age.

Bone shapes relate to, but do not define, function

Long bones, such as the femur and humerus, are longer than they are wide and often act as levers. Short bones, including many carpals and tarsals, provide support with limited motion. Flat bones such as ribs, sternum, and many skull bones can protect organs and offer broad surfaces for muscle attachment. Irregular bones have complex shapes, as seen in vertebrae and many facial bones. Sesamoid bones develop within some tendons and can alter the direction or distribution of force.

Shape categories are a study aid rather than a strict measure of size. A “long bone” is defined by its shape, not simply by being large. Sesamoid bone number can vary, and not every small rounded bone should be counted as a standard element in a fixed inventory. Relating each type to its location, surface features, and articulation gives more context than memorizing a label alone.

  • Long: shaft and expanded ends; many serve as levers.
  • Short: compact proportions; common in the wrist and ankle.
  • Flat: thin, often curved plates; protect or provide attachment area.
  • Irregular: complex geometry; includes vertebrae and many facial bones.
  • Sesamoid: embedded in tendons; size and presence can vary.

Bone tissue is organized at several scales

The outer surface of most bone is covered by periosteum, a connective-tissue membrane that carries vessels and provides attachment for tendons and ligaments. Compact bone is dense and organized into osteons in many regions. Spongy bone contains trabeculae arranged along lines of stress, with marrow spaces between them. Articular cartilage covers joint surfaces where bones meet; it reduces friction and helps distribute load, but it is not a bone layer.

Bone matrix combines mineral salts, especially calcium phosphate, with collagen and other organic components. The mineral contributes stiffness, while collagen helps resist tensile forces. Osteoblasts form new matrix, osteocytes maintain the surrounding tissue, and osteoclasts resorb bone. These cell populations coordinate continuous remodeling. The skeleton is therefore metabolically active; it stores minerals and adapts over time rather than functioning as an inert frame.

Joints connect bones and set movement options

A joint or articulation is a location where bones or other skeletal elements meet. Fibrous joints are connected by dense connective tissue and generally allow little movement. Cartilaginous joints use cartilage between bones and vary in mobility. Synovial joints have a cavity containing synovial fluid, articular cartilage, a capsule, and supporting ligaments; their shapes allow movement such as flexion, extension, rotation, or gliding.

Muscles do not attach directly to every joint surface. Tendons transmit muscle force to bone, while ligaments connect bones and help stabilize joints. A joint’s range depends on its geometry, surrounding soft tissue, and muscle control. A diagram that labels bones alone can miss these relationships, so skeletal study is strongest when paired with joint anatomy and movement terms.

Marrow and remodeling add physiological context

Bone marrow occupies spaces within the skeleton. Red marrow is a site of blood-cell production in selected bones and locations, while yellow marrow contains a larger proportion of fat in many adult long-bone cavities. Distribution changes with age. The marrow is supported by bone architecture and blood supply; it is not simply a hollow space inside every bone.

Remodeling couples resorption and formation, helping renew matrix and respond to mechanical demands. The process also participates in mineral homeostasis. This overview cannot explain an individual’s bone density or diagnose a fracture; those questions need clinical information. For anatomy learning, trace one example from bone surface to compact or trabecular tissue, marrow space, and neighboring joint.

Questions people ask

Frequently asked questions

How many bones are in the adult human skeleton?

A commonly taught standard adult skeleton contains 206 bones. Some bones fuse during development, and variable sesamoid or accessory bones mean that individual counts can differ from the reference convention.

What is the difference between axial and appendicular skeleton?

The axial skeleton forms the central axis, including the skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum. The appendicular skeleton includes the limbs and the girdles that attach them to the trunk.

What is the difference between compact and spongy bone?

Compact bone is dense cortical tissue, while spongy bone is organized as trabeculae with marrow spaces between them. Both are living bone tissue and may be present within one bone.

Are all sesamoid bones included in the 206 count?

The standard count uses a conventional inventory, including some consistently counted bones but not every variable sesamoid. Small tendon-embedded bones can vary between people, so the total is a reference count.

What is the role of bone marrow?

Red marrow produces blood cells in selected skeletal sites. Yellow marrow contains more fat in many adult long-bone cavities. The distribution and proportion of marrow change with age and location.
