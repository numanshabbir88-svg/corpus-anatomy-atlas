Liver Anatomy: Lobes, Lobules, Blood Flow and Bile

Digestive · Liver · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

AnatomyStudy GuideFrequently Asked Questions

The liver is a large metabolic organ in the upper right abdomen. Its microscopic organization allows blood arriving from the digestive tract to pass close to hepatocytes, while bile produced by those cells drains through a separate duct system. This guide follows the liver from its major regions to the flow of blood and bile through a hepatic lobule.

Location, surfaces, and lobes

The liver lies mainly beneath the right dome of the diaphragm, with part extending across the midline. Its diaphragmatic surface follows the diaphragm; its visceral surface faces abdominal organs and carries important impressions and the porta hepatis. Anatomical descriptions commonly identify right and left lobes, with caudate and quadrate regions on the visceral surface. Functional divisions used in surgery follow vascular and biliary territories rather than only the surface landmarks.

The porta hepatis and portal triad

The porta hepatis is a gateway on the visceral surface. The hepatic portal vein brings nutrient-rich blood from much of the gastrointestinal tract and spleen; the hepatic artery brings oxygenated blood. Branches of these vessels travel with bile ducts in portal tracts. Together, a small portal venous branch, hepatic arterial branch, and bile duct are commonly described as components of a portal triad.

Hepatic lobules and sinusoids

A classical hepatic lobule is a useful microscopic model centered on a central vein, with plates of hepatocytes and sinusoids radiating toward it. Sinusoids are specialized vascular channels where portal and arterial blood mix and pass alongside hepatocytes. Kupffer cells, specialized resident macrophages, are associated with the sinusoidal lining. The liver also has other functional models, including portal lobules and acini, each emphasizing different relationships.

Opposing paths of blood and bile

Blood generally flows from portal regions through sinusoids toward central veins, then into hepatic veins and the inferior vena cava. Bile made by hepatocytes enters small canaliculi and flows toward bile ductules and ducts in portal regions—generally opposite the direction of sinusoidal blood flow. Bile eventually leaves the liver through hepatic ducts and can pass into the gallbladder or intestine through the biliary tree.

What the liver does

The liver processes absorbed nutrients, produces many plasma proteins, makes bile, stores selected nutrients, and metabolizes or clears many substances. These jobs depend on blood supply and hepatocyte organization. Anatomy supports function, but this educational overview cannot assess an individual person’s liver health.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where is the liver located?

It lies mainly in the upper right abdomen, immediately below the diaphragm, and extends toward the midline.

What is the porta hepatis?

It is a gateway on the visceral surface where major vessels, nerves, lymphatics, and hepatic ducts enter or leave the liver.

What structures make up the portal triad?

A typical portal triad includes a branch of the hepatic portal vein, a branch of the hepatic artery, and a bile duct within a portal tract.

Do blood and bile flow in the same direction through a lobule?

Generally, no. Blood flows from portal regions through sinusoids toward a central vein, while bile flows through canaliculi toward bile ducts in portal regions.

What is a hepatic sinusoid?

It is a specialized vascular channel between plates of hepatocytes. Blood from portal and arterial branches passes through sinusoids toward central veins.
