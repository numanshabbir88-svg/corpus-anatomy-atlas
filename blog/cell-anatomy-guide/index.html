Human Cell Anatomy: Organelles and Their Functions

Foundations · Cell Biology · Updated November 2026 · 7 min read

AnatomyStudy GuideFrequently Asked Questions

A human cell is a small, organized living system. Its membrane controls exchange with the environment, while internal compartments coordinate genetic instructions, energy production, protein synthesis, recycling, and shape. This guide introduces the main structures found in a typical human cell and explains how they work together. Cell types differ, so not every cell contains every organelle in the same form or amount.

The plasma membrane

The plasma membrane is a flexible boundary built mainly from a phospholipid bilayer with embedded proteins. It separates intracellular fluid from the extracellular environment while allowing selective movement of water, ions, nutrients, and waste. Channels, carriers, pumps, and receptors give the membrane many of its transport and signaling functions.

Nucleus and genetic material

Most human cells contain a nucleus enclosed by a double nuclear envelope. DNA is packaged with proteins as chromatin; specific DNA regions can be transcribed into RNA. Nuclear pores regulate traffic between the nucleus and cytoplasm. Mature red blood cells are an important exception: they lose the nucleus as they develop.

Ribosomes and the endoplasmic reticulum

Ribosomes assemble amino acids into proteins by reading messenger RNA. Free ribosomes mainly make proteins used in the cytosol, while ribosomes attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum make many secreted, membrane, or organelle-targeted proteins. Smooth ER has no attached ribosomes and participates in lipid synthesis, calcium handling, and chemical processing.

Golgi apparatus and vesicles

The Golgi apparatus modifies, sorts, and packages many proteins and lipids arriving from the ER. Membrane-bound vesicles carry this cargo to the cell surface, to other compartments, or outside the cell. Sorting signals help route molecules to the correct destination.

Mitochondria, lysosomes, and the cytoskeleton

Mitochondria convert energy from nutrients into ATP through cellular respiration and also participate in metabolic signaling. Lysosomes contain enzymes that break down selected materials for recycling. The cytoskeleton—microtubules, actin filaments, and intermediate filaments—supports cell shape, internal transport, movement, and division. These structures cooperate rather than operating as isolated parts.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the basic unit of the human body?

The cell is the smallest structural and functional living unit of the human body. Cells specialize and work together to form tissues and organs.

What does the cell membrane do?

It forms a selective boundary, helps regulate movement of substances, and carries receptors and other proteins involved in cell communication.

Which organelle makes most cellular ATP?

Mitochondria produce much of the ATP used by many human cells, although the amount varies by cell type and energy demand.

What is the difference between rough and smooth ER?

Rough ER has ribosomes and helps make and process many proteins. Smooth ER lacks ribosomes and contributes to lipid synthesis, calcium storage, and other metabolic processes.

Do all human cells have a nucleus?

No. For example, mature red blood cells lack a nucleus. Most other human cells have one, and some specialized cells can contain multiple nuclei.
