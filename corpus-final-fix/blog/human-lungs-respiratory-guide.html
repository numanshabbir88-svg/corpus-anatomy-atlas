Human Lungs: Structure, Airways & How Breathing Works

Respiratory System · Lungs · Breathing · Updated November 2026
Alveoli Bronchi Diaphragm Gas exchange

The human lungs are the primary organs of the respiratory system. They take in oxygen from the air and expel carbon dioxide — a process called gas exchange. An average adult takes about 12–20 breaths per minute, and over a lifetime, the lungs process millions of liters of air. This guide covers lung anatomy, the airway tree, breathing mechanics, and common respiratory concepts.

1. Lung Anatomy: Right vs Left

  • Right lung: Larger; divided into 3 lobes (upper, middle, lower).
  • Left lung: Slightly smaller (to make room for the heart); divided into 2 lobes (upper, lower).
  • Pleura: Double-layered membrane surrounding each lung; pleural fluid reduces friction during breathing.
  • Hilum: The central region where bronchi, blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatics enter and exit.

2. The Airway Tree (Conducting Zone)

Air travels through a branching system before reaching the gas-exchange surface:

  • Nose/Nasal cavity → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Terminal bronchioles
  • The trachea splits into the right and left primary bronchi at the carina.
  • Bronchi further divide into smaller bronchioles, ending in terminal bronchioles.
Air path:
Nose → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Right/Left Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli

3. Alveoli: The Gas-Exchange Surface

  • Approximately 300–500 million alveoli in both lungs combined.
  • Alveoli are tiny air sacs surrounded by capillaries — the site where oxygen enters the blood and CO₂ leaves.
  • Type I pneumocytes: Thin cells forming the gas-exchange barrier.
  • Type II pneumocytes: Produce pulmonary surfactant, which prevents alveolar collapse.

4. Breathing Mechanics

  • Inhalation (inspiration): Diaphragm contracts and flattens; intercostal muscles lift ribs; chest volume increases; pressure drops; air rushes in.
  • Exhalation (expiration): Diaphragm relaxes; chest volume decreases; pressure rises; air is pushed out.
  • Diaphragm: Dome-shaped muscle at the base of the thorax; the main muscle of breathing.

5. Lung Volumes & Capacities

TermMeaning
Tidal volumeAir inhaled/exhaled in a normal breath (~500 mL)
Vital capacityMaximum air exhaled after maximum inhalation (~3,500–4,500 mL)
Residual volumeAir remaining after maximum exhalation (~1,200 mL)

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1. How many lobes does each lung have?
Right lung: 3 lobes. Left lung: 2 lobes.
Q2. Where does gas exchange occur?
In the alveoli — tiny air sacs surrounded by capillaries.
Q3. What is the main muscle of breathing?
The diaphragm — it contracts and flattens during inhalation.
Q4. What does surfactant do?
Pulmonary surfactant (from Type II pneumocytes) reduces surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse.
Q5. What is tidal volume?
The volume of air inhaled or exhaled in a normal breath, about 500 mL.
