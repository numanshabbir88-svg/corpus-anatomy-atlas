Egg cell anatomy: oocyte and ovulation

Overview

How this part of the body works

An oocyte begins development before birth as a primary oocyte arrested in meiosis I. During a reproductive cycle, an LH surge triggers completion of meiosis I in a selected follicle; ovulation releases a secondary oocyte arrested in meiosis II, surrounded by zona pellucida and corona radiata. Meiosis II usually finishes only after sperm entry, creating the ovum and another polar body. The follicle becomes a progesterone-secreting corpus luteum. A secondary oocyte in this teaching cutaway has no large resting central nucleus; the small polar body is outside the oocyte membrane but inside the zona. This is a stylized microscopic-scale model, not an actual ovum photograph.

Read the anatomy, part by part

These 8 structures describe anatomy related to the chapter’s regional illustration. Each entry separates location, function and a clinical point.

01

Secondary oocyte

Large haploid-stage cell released at ovulation, arrested in metaphase II until fertilization.

What it doesContributes maternal genetic material when fertilization occurs.

Clinical connectionThe illustrated cytoplasm is not a distinct mature nucleus.

Picture context Ovulated-cell cutaway: zona, corona and small polar body; not a clinical oocyte image.

02

Zona pellucida

Extracellular glycoprotein layer immediately outside oocyte cell membrane and inside corona radiata.

What it doesSupports sperm recognition and protects the developing conceptus early.

Clinical connectionIt is not the oocyte nucleus or an ovarian follicle wall.

Picture context Ovulated-cell cutaway: zona, corona and small polar body; not a clinical oocyte image.

03

Corona radiata

Layer of granulosa-derived cells closely surrounds zona pellucida after ovulation.

What it doesProvides support around the released oocyte.

Clinical connectionThe individual cells in the digital cutaway are illustrative, not a diagnostic slide.

First polar body

Small meiotic by-product sits in perivitelline space between the oocyte membrane and zona pellucida.

What it doesReflects unequal cytoplasmic division during oogenesis.

Clinical connectionDo not confuse it with a second oocyte or early embryo.

05

Perivitelline space

Narrow space between oocyte membrane and zona pellucida, where a polar body may lie.

What it doesSeparates cell surface from extracellular coat.

Clinical connectionA drawing may exaggerate this very small gap.

06

Oocyte plasma membrane

Oolemma surrounds the oocyte cytoplasm beneath the zona pellucida.

What it doesParticipates in sperm–oocyte membrane interaction.

Clinical connectionThe zona and membrane are distinct boundaries.

Ooplasm contains maternal organelles and factors needed for early divisions.

Meiotic apparatus lies near cell periphery in the unfertilized secondary oocyte.

Questions people ask

Five questions about Egg cell · oocyte & ovulation

Short answers grounded in the structures described above. Use the illustrated guide for anatomical context.

Is the egg released at ovulation already a mature ovum?

Typically the released cell is a secondary oocyte arrested in meiosis II, not a completed ovum. Meiosis II normally finishes only after sperm entry.

What surrounds the ovulated human oocyte?

An extracellular zona pellucida surrounds its plasma membrane, and granulosa-derived corona radiata cells surround the zona. These structures are distinct from the ovarian follicle wall left behind.

What is the first polar body?

It is a small by-product of unequal division during completion of oocyte meiosis I. It can lie in the perivitelline space between the oocyte membrane and zona pellucida; it is not a second egg.

When do human primary oocytes form?

Primary oocytes arise before birth in the fetal ovary and remain arrested in meiosis I until selected cycles years later. In a typical cycle an LH surge precedes the final preovulatory meiotic transition.

What happens to the follicle after ovulation?

The follicle remnant becomes a corpus luteum that produces progesterone and estrogen. If implantation follows, early trophoblast hCG helps maintain that luteal hormone support.
