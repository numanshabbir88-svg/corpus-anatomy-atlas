Overview

How this part of the body works

Every smile, blink, and raised eyebrow depends on the seventh cranial nerve—a complex mixed nerve that threads through the temporal bone before fanning across the face. CN VII contains motor fibers to muscles of facial expression, taste from anterior two-thirds of tongue (via chorda tympani), and parasympathetic pathways for lacrimal, submandibular and sublingual glands. It enters the internal acoustic meatus, bends at geniculate ganglion, traverses the facial canal and exits at stylomastoid foramen. Within the parotid it forms a motor plexus giving temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal mandibular and cervical branches; it passes through the gland but does not provide its secretomotor supply. Within the human body, the facial nerve · cn vii (Nervus facialis) is situated in pons → internal acoustic meatus → facial canal → stylomastoid foramen → parotid plexus, receives arterial perfusion via AICA/labyrinthine and petrosal branches intracranially; stylomastoid artery distally, and is innervated by CN VII motor, parasympathetic and taste fibers; cutaneous face sensation mainly CN V.

Structurally, the facial motor nucleus (Nucleus nervi facialis) comprises motor cell bodies in pons send axons looping around abducens nucleus before exit. Physiologically, the facial motor nucleus controls muscles of facial expression. Structurally, the nervus intermedius comprises sensory and parasympathetic root accompanying the motor root. Physiologically, the nervus intermedius carries taste and secretomotor pathways. Structurally, the internal acoustic meatus (Meatus acusticus internus) comprises temporal-bone canal shared by CN VII, CN VIII and labyrinthine artery. Physiologically, the internal acoustic meatus governs conveys nerve toward facial canal. Structurally, the geniculate ganglion (Ganglion geniculi) comprises sensory ganglion at first sharp bend of facial canal; greater petrosal branch arises nearby. Physiologically, the geniculate ganglion houses sensory neuron cell bodies. The greater petrosal nerve (Nervus petrosus major) carries preganglionic fibers toward pterygopalatine ganglion. Physiologically, the greater petrosal nerve supports lacrimal and nasal/palatine gland secretion. Structurally, the nerve to stapedius (Nervus stapedius) comprises branch in facial canal to stapedius muscle of middle ear. Physiologically, the nerve to stapedius governs dampens stapes movement.

Clinically, regarding the facial motor nucleus, upper face often retains bilateral cortical input in supranuclear lesions. From a diagnostic standpoint, in the nervus intermedius, explains why CN VII is not solely motor. In clinical pathology involving the internal acoustic meatus, lesions here may affect facial movement and hearing/balance. Medical evaluation of the geniculate ganglion also notes that herpes zoster may affect geniculate region.

Ganglion geniculi

Greater petrosal nerve

Nervus petrosus major

Carries preganglionic fibers toward pterygopalatine ganglion.

What it doesSupports lacrimal and nasal/palatine gland secretion.

Nerve to stapedius

Nervus stapedius

Branch in facial canal to stapedius muscle of middle ear.

What it doesDampens stapes movement.

Clinical connectionProximal lesion can produce hyperacusis.

07

Chorda tympani

Crosses middle ear then joins lingual nerve (V3).

What it doesTaste from anterior two-thirds of tongue; submandibular ganglion secretomotor input.

Clinical connectionCan be injured by middle-ear disease or surgery.

08

Stylomastoid foramen

Foramen stylomastoideum

Exit of CN VII from temporal bone behind ear.

What it doesDelivers motor trunk to face and neck.

Clinical connectionDistal lesions spare proximal taste/lacrimal branches.

09

Posterior auricular branch

Nervus auricularis posterior

Early extracranial branch to auricular muscles and occipitalis.

What it doesMoves posterior auricle and posterior scalp.

Clinical connectionArises before parotid plexus.

10

Parotid plexus

Plexus intraparotideus

Branching motor network within parotid gland.

What it doesDistributes facial expression motor fibers.

Clinical connectionParotid secretion is mainly CN IX parasympathetic, not CN VII.

11

Temporal branch

Rami temporales

Superior terminal branches to frontalis and superior orbicularis oculi.

What it doesRaises brow and closes upper eyelid.

Clinical connectionForehead weakness helps localize lower motor neuron injury.

12

Zygomatic branch

Rami zygomatici

Branches across zygomatic region to orbicularis oculi and neighboring muscles.

What it doesCloses eye and coordinates upper facial expression.

Clinical connectionCorneal blink efferent limb is CN VII; afferent is V1.

13

Buccal branch

Rami buccales

Motor branches to buccinator, orbicularis oris and upper lip muscles.

What it doesHelps seal lips and move cheek.

Clinical connectionNot the buccal sensory nerve of mandibular CN V3.

14

Marginal mandibular branch

Ramus marginalis mandibulae

Branch along mandibular margin to depressors of lower lip/chin.

What it doesShapes lower-lip expressions.

Clinical connectionAt risk during submandibular or mandibular surgery.

15

Cervical branch

Ramus colli

Inferior branch descending to platysma.

What it doesTenses superficial neck skin.

Clinical connectionDeep facial sensation remains principally trigeminal.

16

Facial sensation · CN V

Nervus trigeminus

V1/V2/V3 provide most cutaneous sensory input from face, unlike VII expression branches.

What it doesSeparates sensory testing from facial movement testing.

Clinical connectionDo not diagnose trigeminal sensory loss from a VII motor lesion alone.

17

Facial colliculus

Dorsal pontine prominence formed partly by facial nerve fibers looping around abducens nucleus.

What it doesMarks internal VII course in fourth-ventricle floor.

Clinical connectionPontine lesion may combine VI and VII signs.

18

Digastric branch of VII

Early motor branch after stylomastoid exit toward posterior belly of digastric.

What it doesSupports jaw opening and hyoid elevation.

Clinical connectionBranches before intraparotid terminal divisions.

19

Stylohyoid branch

Motor branch of facial nerve to stylohyoid muscle.

What it doesElevates and retracts hyoid during swallowing.

Clinical connectionArises proximal to main facial expression branches.

20

Submandibular ganglion pathway

Chorda tympani fibers travel with lingual V3 to submandibular ganglion.

What it doesPostganglionic fibers stimulate submandibular/sublingual secretion.

Clinical connectionTaste fibers pass through without synapsing there.

Questions people ask

Five questions about Facial nerve · CN VII

Short answers grounded in the structures described above. Use the illustrated guide for anatomical context.

What does the facial nerve control?

Cranial nerve VII supplies muscles of facial expression and also carries taste and parasympathetic fibers through specific branches. The parotid plexus (Plexus intraparotideus) is branching motor network within parotid gland. Physiologically, the parotid plexus distributes facial expression motor fibers.

Does the facial nerve provide most facial skin sensation?

No. Most facial skin sensation travels in branches of the trigeminal nerve; the facial nerve is mainly known for its motor supply to facial expression. Structurally, the facial sensation · CN V (Nervus trigeminus) comprises v1/V2/V3 provide most cutaneous sensory input from face, unlike VII expression branches. Physiologically, the facial sensation · CN V separates sensory testing from facial movement testing.

What is the facial motor nucleus?

Motor cell bodies in pons send axons looping around abducens nucleus before exit. Functionally, it controls muscles of facial expression. In clinical pathology involving the facial motor nucleus, upper face often retains bilateral cortical input in supranuclear lesions.

Where is the internal acoustic meatus located?

Temporal-bone canal shared by CN VII, CN VIII and labyrinthine artery. Functionally, it governs conveys nerve toward facial canal. Medical evaluation of the internal acoustic meatus also notes that lesions here may affect facial movement and hearing/balance.

What is the function of the greater petrosal nerve?

Functionally, it supports lacrimal and nasal/palatine gland secretion. Carries preganglionic fibers toward pterygopalatine ganglion. Clinically, regarding the greater petrosal nerve, a lesion proximal to branch can impair lacrimation.
