Overview

How this part of the body works

Beneath the ribcage, two spongy, elastic lungs branch through more than twenty generations of airways to bring fresh air within a fraction of a micron of blood. Air is conducted from the trachea through progressively dividing bronchi and bronchioles until it reaches the alveoli, where oxygen diffuses into pulmonary capillaries and carbon dioxide diffuses out. The right lung has three lobes separated by oblique and horizontal fissures; the left has two, with a cardiac notch and lingula to accommodate the heart. Each lung is wrapped in visceral pleura, opposed by parietal pleura across a thin fluid-filled cavity whose surface tension keeps the lung expanded. Within the human body, the lungs (Pulmones) is situated in pleural cavities of the thorax, either side of the mediastinum, receives arterial perfusion via pulmonary arteries (gas exchange) + bronchial arteries (tissue supply), and is innervated by pulmonary plexus — vagus (bronchoconstriction) + sympathetic T2–T4.

The trachea is a 10–12 cm tube held open by 16–20 C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings, open posteriorly where the trachealis muscle lies. Lined by ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium with goblet cells. Physiologically, the trachea conducts, warms, humidifies and filters inspired air. The main & lobar bronchi (Bronchi principales et lobares) is the trachea divides at the carina (T4/T5) into right and left main bronchi, which divide into lobar then segmental bronchi. Cartilage plates replace rings as the airways narrow. Physiologically, the main & lobar bronchi governs distribute air to each lobe and bronchopulmonary segment. Structurally, the bronchioles (Bronchioli) comprises airways under 1 mm with no cartilage and relatively more smooth muscle, ending in terminal then respiratory bronchioles. Physiologically, the bronchioles regulate airflow resistance and deliver air to the alveolar ducts. The right lung — three lobes (Pulmo dexter) are superior, middle and inferior lobes separated by the oblique and horizontal fissures. Shorter than the left because the liver sits below, but wider and heavier. Physiologically, the right lung — three lobes governs gas exchange across ~150 million alveoli. The left lung — two lobes (Pulmo sinister) are superior and inferior lobes separated by the oblique fissure only. Narrower than the right because the heart bulges to the left. Physiologically, the left lung — two lobes governs gas exchange; the lingula of the superior lobe is the equivalent of the right middle lobe. The fissures (Fissurae pulmonis) are deep invaginations of visceral pleura. The oblique fissure runs in both lungs (T4 posteriorly to the 6th rib anteriorly); the horizontal fissure is right-sided only. Physiologically, the fissures allow the lobes to slide against each other and define surgical planes.

Clinically, regarding the trachea, foreign bodies more often enter the right main bronchus because it is wider, shorter and more vertical. From a diagnostic standpoint, in the main & lobar bronchi, each segmental bronchus supplies a bronchopulmonary segment that can be surgically removed on its own. In clinical pathology involving the bronchioles, smooth-muscle spasm of the bronchioles is the main problem in asthma; smooth muscle is also the target of salbutamol. Medical evaluation of the right lung — three lobes also notes that the right middle lobe is prone to collapse (middle-lobe syndrome) because its bronchus is narrow and surrounded by nodes.

Illustrated structures

Read the anatomy, part by part

These 21 structures describe anatomy related to the chapter’s regional illustration. Each entry separates location, function and a clinical point.

01

Trachea

A 10–12 cm tube held open by 16–20 C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings, open posteriorly where the trachealis muscle lies. Lined by ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium with goblet cells.

What it doesConducts, warms, humidifies and filters inspired air.

Clinical connectionForeign bodies more often enter the right main bronchus because it is wider, shorter and more vertical.

02

Main & lobar bronchi

Bronchi principales et lobares

The trachea divides at the carina (T4/T5) into right and left main bronchi, which divide into lobar then segmental bronchi. Cartilage plates replace rings as the airways narrow.

What it doesDistribute air to each lobe and bronchopulmonary segment.

Clinical connectionEach segmental bronchus supplies a bronchopulmonary segment that can be surgically removed on its own.

03

Bronchioles

Bronchioli

Airways under 1 mm with no cartilage and relatively more smooth muscle, ending in terminal then respiratory bronchioles.

What it doesRegulate airflow resistance and deliver air to the alveolar ducts.

Clinical connectionSmooth-muscle spasm of the bronchioles is the main problem in asthma; smooth muscle is also the target of salbutamol.

04

Right lung — three lobes

Pulmo dexter

Superior, middle and inferior lobes separated by the oblique and horizontal fissures. Shorter than the left because the liver sits below, but wider and heavier.

What it doesGas exchange across ~150 million alveoli.

Clinical connectionThe right middle lobe is prone to collapse (middle-lobe syndrome) because its bronchus is narrow and surrounded by nodes.

05

Left lung — two lobes

Pulmo sinister

Superior and inferior lobes separated by the oblique fissure only. Narrower than the right because the heart bulges to the left.

What it doesGas exchange; the lingula of the superior lobe is the equivalent of the right middle lobe.

Clinical connectionA left upper-lobe mass can compress the recurrent laryngeal nerve, causing hoarseness.

06

Fissures

Fissurae pulmonis

Deep invaginations of visceral pleura. The oblique fissure runs in both lungs (T4 posteriorly to the 6th rib anteriorly); the horizontal fissure is right-sided only.

What it doesAllow the lobes to slide against each other and define surgical planes.

Clinical connectionFluid collects in the fissures on a chest radiograph as a "phantom tumour" in heart failure.

07

Cardiac notch & lingula

Incisura cardiaca

A concave impression on the anterior border of the left lung that cradles the heart; the tongue-like projection below it is the lingula.

What it doesMakes room for the heart and left ventricular apex.

Clinical connectionThe bare area of pericardium behind this notch allows pericardiocentesis without crossing the pleura.

08

Hilum

Hilum pulmonis

The wedge-shaped entry point on the mediastinal surface: main bronchus, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, bronchial vessels, lymphatics and nerves.

What it doesThe lung's root — everything enters and leaves here.

Clinical connectionHilar lymph node enlargement is a classic sign of sarcoidosis and tuberculosis.

09

Pleura & pleural cavity

Pleura

Visceral pleura covers the lung; parietal pleura lines the wall. Between them is a potential space with a film of serous fluid.

What it doesSurface tension couples the lung to the chest wall so it expands with it, and the fluid lets the layers glide.

Clinical connectionAir entering the cavity collapses the lung — a pneumothorax. Fluid gives a pleural effusion with stony dullness on percussion.

10

Pulmonary arteries

Arteriae pulmonales

Carry deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle, branching alongside the bronchi into each segment.

What it doesBring the whole cardiac output to the alveolar capillaries.

Clinical connectionHypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction shunts blood away from poorly ventilated regions — reversed globally in COPD, causing pulmonary hypertension.

11

Pulmonary veins

Venae pulmonales

Four veins that collect oxygenated blood from the capillary plexus and drain into the left atrium.

What it doesReturn oxygenated blood to the systemic pump.

Clinical connectionThey run in the interlobular septa — the opposite position to the pulmonary arteries.

12

Diaphragm

Diaphragma

A dome-shaped musculotendinous sheet separating thorax from abdomen, with crura arising from the lumbar vertebrae and openings for the aorta (T12), oesophagus (T10) and IVC (T8).

What it doesThe main muscle of inspiration — contraction flattens the dome and enlarges the thorax.

Clinical connectionSupplied by the phrenic nerve (C3–C5): "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive". A high spinal cord injury above C3 stops spontaneous breathing.

13

Alveoli & capillary bed

Alveoli pulmonis

Thin-walled sacs of type I pneumocytes wrapped in capillaries; type II pneumocytes secrete surfactant and alveolar macrophages patrol the surface. Together they present ~70 m² of membrane only 0.5 µm thick.

What it doesDiffusion of O₂ into blood and CO₂ out of it.

Clinical connectionSurfactant deficiency in premature infants causes respiratory distress syndrome. Emphysema destroys the walls, reducing surface area.

14

Terminal bronchiole

Bronchiolus terminalis

Final nonalveolated conducting airway before respiratory zone.

What it doesDelivers air to downstream acinus.

Clinical connectionContains no cartilage plates or submucosal glands.

15

Respiratory bronchiole

Bronchiolus respiratorius

First airway segment with alveoli interrupting its wall.

What it doesCombines air conduction and some gas exchange.

Clinical connectionMarks proximal boundary of acinus.

16

Alveolar duct

Ductus alveolaris

Distal passage whose walls are largely alveolar openings.

What it doesDistributes air to multiple alveolar sacs.

Clinical connectionMore extensively alveolated than respiratory bronchiole.

17

Alveolar sac

Sacculus alveolaris

Common terminal chamber opening into a cluster of alveoli.

What it doesShares airflow among terminal alveoli.

Clinical connectionNot synonymous with a single alveolus.

18

Alveolus

Alveolus pulmonis

Tiny airspace lined by specialized epithelium and surrounded by capillaries.

What it doesProvides extensive diffusion surface.

Clinical connectionDestruction reduces gas-exchange area.

19

Type I pneumocyte

Pneumocytus typus I

Very thin squamous alveolar epithelial cell covering most alveolar surface.

What it doesMinimizes gas diffusion distance.

Clinical connectionDamage can impair barrier integrity.

20

Type II pneumocyte

Pneumocytus typus II

Cuboidal cell with lamellar bodies, commonly at alveolar corners.

What it doesSecretes surfactant and contributes to epithelial renewal.

Clinical connectionImmature surfactant production contributes to neonatal respiratory distress.

21

Alveolar macrophage

Macrophagocytus alveolaris

Resident/mobile phagocyte in alveolar airspaces.

What it doesRemoves particulates and participates in host defense.

Clinical connectionInflammation can recruit further macrophages.

Questions people ask

Five questions about Lungs

Short answers grounded in the structures described above. Use the illustrated guide for anatomical context.

What are the lobes of the right and left lungs?

The right lung usually has superior, middle and inferior lobes; the left has superior and inferior lobes with a cardiac notch. Fissures divide the lobes, though normal anatomy can vary. Physiologically, the right lung — three lobes governs gas exchange across ~150 million alveoli. Clinically, regarding the right lung — three lobes, the right middle lobe is prone to collapse (middle-lobe syndrome) because its bronchus is narrow and surrounded by nodes.

Where does air go after the bronchi?

Air passes from progressively smaller bronchi to bronchioles, then through the respiratory zone toward alveoli. Exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide occurs across the very thin alveolar–capillary barrier. The alveoli & capillary bed (Alveoli pulmonis) is thin-walled sacs of type I pneumocytes wrapped in capillaries; type II pneumocytes secrete surfactant and alveolar macrophages patrol the surface. Together they present ~70 m² of membrane only 0.5 µm thick. Physiologically, the alveoli & capillary bed governs diffusion of O₂ into blood and CO₂ out of it.

What is a Type I pneumocyte?

A Type I pneumocyte is a very thin squamous cell forming much of the alveolar gas-exchange surface. It can be recognized on the separate microscopic-scale teaching illustration, not on the whole-lung image. Structurally, the type I pneumocyte (Pneumocytus typus I) comprises very thin squamous alveolar epithelial cell covering most alveolar surface. Physiologically, the type I pneumocyte governs minimizes gas diffusion distance.

What does a Type II pneumocyte do?

A Type II pneumocyte is a plumper alveolar cell that secretes pulmonary surfactant, which helps reduce surface tension. It also contributes to maintenance and repair of the alveolar lining. Structurally, the type II pneumocyte (Pneumocytus typus II) comprises cuboidal cell with lamellar bodies, commonly at alveolar corners. Physiologically, the type II pneumocyte secretes surfactant and contributes to epithelial renewal.

Is the enlarged alveolar image an actual microscope slide?

No. It is a digital educational illustration that shows the thin Type I lining next to a plumper Type II cell. It is not histology or a literal magnification of the macroscopic lung image.
