Overview

How this part of the body works

Covering roughly two square meters in an average adult, the skin is our largest organ—acting simultaneously as a waterproof shield, radiator, and sensory surface. The epidermis is a keratinising stratified squamous epithelium renewed from the basal layer every ~4 weeks, with melanocytes, Langerhans cells and Merkel cells among the keratinocytes. Beneath it the dermis gives strength and elasticity (papillary loose, reticular dense connective tissue) and carries the vessels, nerves, hair follicles and glands. The hypodermis is fat that insulates and cushions. Skin makes vitamin D, senses the world, and regulates temperature by sweating and by shunting blood through arteriovenous anastomoses. Within the human body, the skin (Cutis) is situated in whole body surface — the largest organ, receives arterial perfusion via dermal plexuses from segmental perforators, and is innervated by somatic sensory (touch, pain, temperature) + sympathetic.

Structurally, the stratum corneum comprises 15–20 layers of dead, flattened, keratin-filled corneocytes held by lipids — the "brick and mortar" barrier. Physiologically, the stratum corneum governs waterproofing and the main barrier to microbes and chemicals. The stratum basale & spinosum (Stratum basale) is the single basal row of dividing keratinocytes on the basement membrane, plus the prickle-cell layers above with desmosomes, melanocytes and Langerhans cells. Physiologically, the stratum basale & spinosum governs renews the epidermis; melanocytes transfer pigment; Langerhans cells present antigen. Structurally, the dermis (papillary & reticular) (Dermis) comprises papillary layer of loose connective tissue forming dermal papillae; reticular layer of dense irregular collagen and elastin. Physiologically, the dermis (papillary & reticular) governs strength, elasticity, sensation and the vascular supply of the epidermis. Structurally, the hypodermis (subcutis) (Tela subcutanea) comprises loose connective tissue and fat, with larger vessels and nerves and, in places, panniculus carnosus. Physiologically, the hypodermis (subcutis) governs insulation, energy store, shock absorption and skin mobility. The hair shaft & follicle (Folliculus pili) is a keratinised shaft growing from a follicle that dips into dermis or fat, with a bulb around a dermal papilla and a bulge of stem cells. Physiologically, the hair shaft & follicle governs protection, sensation and (in other mammals) insulation. The arrector pili muscle (Musculus arrector pili) is a band of smooth muscle from follicle to dermis, supplied by sympathetic fibres. Physiologically, the arrector pili muscle governs erects the hair (goose flesh) and helps express sebum.

Clinically, regarding the stratum corneum, its thickness defines palms and soles; loss in burns allows catastrophic fluid and heat loss. From a diagnostic standpoint, in the stratum basale & spinosum, basal cell carcinoma arises here at sun-exposed sites; melanoma from melanocytes. Clinically, the dermis (papillary & reticular) split-thickness grafts take from the dermal remnants left in the wound bed; keloids are dermal over-scarring. Medical evaluation of the hypodermis (subcutis) also notes that subcutaneous insulin absorption varies with site and temperature because of this layer's blood flow.

Stratum corneum

Stratum basale

Hypodermis (subcutis)

05

Hair shaft & follicle

Folliculus pili

A keratinised shaft growing from a follicle that dips into dermis or fat, with a bulb around a dermal papilla and a bulge of stem cells.

What it doesProtection, sensation and (in other mammals) insulation.

Clinical connectionFollicular stem cells re-epithelialise partial-thickness wounds; follicles are the target of alopecias.

06

Arrector pili muscle

Musculus arrector pili

A band of smooth muscle from follicle to dermis, supplied by sympathetic fibres.

What it doesErects the hair (goose flesh) and helps express sebum.

Clinical connectionIts contraction in cold traps air for insulation — vestigial in humans.

07

Sebaceous gland

Glandula sebacea

A holocrine gland of lipid-filled cells opening into the follicle above the arrector pili insertion.

What it doesSebum lubricates and waterproofs hair and skin and has mild antimicrobial action.

Clinical connectionBlocked, colonised glands are the lesion of acne vulgaris (Propionibacterium/Cutibacterium acnes).

08

Eccrine sweat gland & duct

Glandula sudorifera

A coiled secretory coil deep in the dermis with a duct spiralling through the epidermis to a pore.

What it doesEvaporative cooling — the main human thermoregulatory mechanism; also excretes salt, urea and lactate.

Clinical connectionHeat stroke follows when sweating fails; cystic fibrosis makes sweat abnormally salty (chloride test).

09

Meissner corpuscle

Corpusculum tacti

A stacked, encapsulated ending in the dermal papillae of glabrous skin.

What it doesDetects light touch and low-frequency vibration (rapidly adapting).

Clinical connectionDense in fingertips and lips — the reason two-point discrimination is finest there.

10

Pacinian corpuscle

Corpusculum lamellosum

An onion-like encapsulated receptor in deep dermis and hypodermis.

What it doesSenses deep pressure and high-frequency vibration.

Clinical connectionIts loss in leprosy and diabetes contributes to unnoticed injury and ulceration.

11

Dermal vessels & AV anastomoses

Plexus vasculosus

A deep and a superficial plexus with capillary loops in each dermal papilla, plus glomus arteriovenous shunts in acral skin.

What it doesNutrition of the epidermis and control of heat loss by shunting blood.

Clinical connectionOpening the shunts flushes and cools; closing them in cold makes fingers pale — exaggerated in Raynaud phenomenon.

12

Stratum basale

Deep single proliferative keratinocyte layer adjoining basement membrane.

What it doesRenews overlying epidermis.

Clinical connectionBasal-cell carcinoma derives from basal-type cells.

13

Stratum spinosum

Several layers of keratinocytes strongly joined by desmosomes.

What it doesProvides mechanical cohesion.

Clinical connectionDesmosomal disruption causes epidermal blistering.

14

Stratum granulosum

Flattened keratinocytes with keratohyalin granules and lipid-releasing lamellar bodies.

What it doesBuilds the water-permeability barrier.

Clinical connectionBarrier injury increases transepidermal water loss.

15

Stratum lucidum

Thin translucent transition zone most apparent in glabrous palms and soles.

What it doesAdds specialized layer in thick skin.

Clinical connectionDo not expect a distinct lucidum in ordinary thin hairy skin.

16

Basement membrane zone

Junctio dermoepidermalis

Anchoring complex joining epidermis to dermis with hemidesmosomes and matrix.

What it doesResists separation and supports renewal.

Clinical connectionPlane of separation distinguishes blister disorders.

17

Dermal papillae

Papillae dermis

Fingerlike papillary dermal projections interlocking with epidermal ridges.

What it doesIncrease attachment surface and house capillary loops.

Clinical connectionRete ridge pattern varies by body site.

18

Papillary dermis

Stratum papillare dermis

Superficial loose connective tissue with capillaries and fine collagen.

What it doesNourishes avascular epidermis by diffusion.

Clinical connectionSuperficial wounds may preserve deep appendages.

19

Reticular dermis

Stratum reticulare dermis

Deeper dense irregular collagen-and-elastin connective tissue.

What it doesProvides tensile strength and elasticity.

Clinical connectionFull-thickness injury through reticular dermis commonly scars.

20

Hypodermis

Tela subcutanea

Adipose lobules and connective septa deep to the dermis.

What it doesInsulation, cushioning and movable attachment.

Clinical connectionNot one of the histologic dermal layers.

Questions people ask

Five questions about Skin

Short answers grounded in the structures described above. Use the illustrated guide for anatomical context.

What are the main layers of the skin?

The epidermis is the outer epithelial barrier; the vascular dermis contains connective tissue, vessels and nerves. Subcutaneous tissue lies beneath the skin and varies by region.

How does skin help protect the body?

The keratinized epidermal surface limits water loss and helps block external injury and microbes, while deeper tissue supports sensation, repair and temperature regulation.

What is the stratum corneum?

The stratum corneum is the outer epidermal layer, formed by flattened dead keratinocytes held together by intercellular lipids.

Where are the papillary and reticular dermis layers?

The dermis lies beneath the epidermis and above the hypodermis; its papillary layer is superficial and its reticular layer is deeper.

What roles do the hair shaft and follicle have?

Hair shafts provide protection and sensation; hair follicles anchor and produce the hair.
