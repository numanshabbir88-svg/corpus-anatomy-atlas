Overview

How this part of the body works

Protected beneath ribs nine through eleven in the upper left abdomen, the spleen is the largest lymphoid organ in the body and a precision filter for circulating blood. Blood enters the spleen through the splenic artery and percolates through two compartments. White pulp — cuffs of lymphocytes around central arterioles — mounts immune responses to blood-borne antigens, making IgM and opsonins. Red pulp is a mesh of sinusoids and cords where old, stiff or antibody-coated red cells are trapped and destroyed by macrophages, and where platelets are stored (a third of the body's pool). In fetal life the spleen makes red cells; after splenectomy the liver and marrow take over, but the loss of opsonisation leaves lifelong risk of encapsulated bacterial sepsis. Within the human body, the spleen (Lien (splen)) is situated in left hypochondrium, ribs 9–11, posterior to the stomach, receives arterial perfusion via splenic artery (largest branch of the coeliac trunk), and is innervated by coeliac plexus (sympathetic) — mostly vasomotor.

The capsule & trabeculae (Capsula lienis) is a fibroelastic capsule sending trabeculae into the pulp, carrying the vessels. Physiologically, the capsule & trabeculae governs structural framework and conduit for vessels. Structurally, the red pulp (Pulpa rubra) comprises splenic cords of Billroth and wide sinusoids lined by stave cells with slit-like gaps. Physiologically, the red pulp filters senescent and abnormal red cells; recycles iron; stores platelets and monocytes. Structurally, the white pulp (Pulpa alba) comprises periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths (T cells), follicles (B cells) and a marginal zone rich in macrophages. Physiologically, the white pulp governs immune surveillance of blood: antibody and opsonin production, especially against capsules. Structurally, the splenic sinusoids (Sinus splenicus) comprises discontinuous, leaky channels whose endothelial slits force red cells to squeeze through. Physiologically, the splenic sinusoids governs the mechanical filter: healthy deformable cells pass; rigid ones are eaten. The hilum (Hilum lienis) is the notched medial border where splenic vessels enter and the tail of pancreas and stomach fundus relate. Physiologically, the hilum governs vascular gateway; also the site of accessory spleens in ~15% of people. The splenic artery (Arteria lienalis) is a tortuous branch of the coeliac trunk running along the upper border of the pancreas. Physiologically, the splenic artery supplies the spleen, pancreas (via branches) and part of the stomach.

Clinically, regarding the capsule & trabeculae, the capsule is thin and the pulp fragile — the spleen tears easily in blunt abdominal trauma and bleeds briskly. From a diagnostic standpoint, in the red pulp, in hereditary spherocytosis the rigid spheres are trapped here, causing haemolytic anaemia cured by splenectomy. In clinical pathology involving the white pulp, its loss explains why asplenic patients are vulnerable to pneumococcus, meningococcus and H. influenzae. Medical evaluation of the splenic sinusoids also notes that malaria parasites stiffen red cells, which is why the spleen enlarges in chronic infection.

Capsule & trabeculae

Capsula lienis

A fibroelastic capsule sending trabeculae into the pulp, carrying the vessels.

Red pulp

Pulpa rubra

White pulp

Pulpa alba

Periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths (T cells), follicles (B cells) and a marginal zone rich in macrophages.

What it doesImmune surveillance of blood: antibody and opsonin production, especially against capsules.

Clinical connectionIts loss explains why asplenic patients are vulnerable to pneumococcus, meningococcus and H. influenzae.

04

Splenic sinusoids

Sinus splenicus

Discontinuous, leaky channels whose endothelial slits force red cells to squeeze through.

What it doesThe mechanical filter: healthy deformable cells pass; rigid ones are eaten.

Clinical connectionMalaria parasites stiffen red cells, which is why the spleen enlarges in chronic infection.

05

Hilum

Hilum lienis

The notched medial border where splenic vessels enter and the tail of pancreas and stomach fundus relate.

What it doesVascular gateway; also the site of accessory spleens in ~15% of people.

Clinical connectionA missed accessory spleen can cause relapse of disease after splenectomy for ITP.

06

Splenic artery

Arteria lienalis

A tortuous branch of the coeliac trunk running along the upper border of the pancreas.

What it doesSupplies the spleen, pancreas (via branches) and part of the stomach.

Clinical connectionIts tortuosity makes it prone to aneurysm in pregnancy and portal hypertension.

07

Splenic vein

Vena lienalis

Drains the spleen and joins the superior mesenteric vein behind the pancreatic neck to form the portal vein.

What it doesReturns filtered blood to the portal system.

Clinical connectionIts thrombosis causes left-sided (sinistral) portal hypertension with isolated gastric varices.

08

Germinal centre

Centrum germinale

The pale core of an activated follicle where B cells proliferate and affinity-mature their antibodies.

What it doesProduces high-affinity, memory B cells against blood-borne antigens.

Clinical connectionVaccines against encapsulated organisms work partly by priming these centres before splenic loss.

09

Periarteriolar lymphoid sheath

T-cell-rich white-pulp cylinder surrounding a central arteriole.

What it doesSamples blood-borne antigens with nearby immune cells.

Clinical connectionDisrupted white pulp weakens adaptive responses.

10

B-cell follicle

Lymphoid nodule adjacent to central arterioles in white pulp.

What it doesSupports B-cell activation and antibody maturation.

Clinical connectionA germinal center appears after antigenic stimulation.

11

Marginal zone

Interface between white and red pulp with specialized macrophages and B cells.

What it doesTraps circulating antigens, including encapsulated bacteria.

Clinical connectionLoss of splenic function increases sepsis risk.

12

Splenic cords

Cell-rich Billroth cords of red pulp between sinusoids.

What it doesFilter circulating erythrocytes and platelets.

Clinical connectionAbnormal red-cell deformability leads to trapping.

13

Sinusoidal slits

Gaps between elongated endothelial stave cells in splenic sinusoids.

What it doesAllow deformable red cells to re-enter circulation.

Clinical connectionRigid or senescent red cells may fail to pass.

14

Central arteriole

Arteriole coursing eccentrically through white pulp.

What it doesBrings blood to immune surveillance regions.

Clinical connectionIt is central to a follicle-associated sheath, not the whole spleen.

15

Penicillar arterioles

Small branches departing splenic white pulp toward red pulp.

What it doesFeed terminal capillaries and splenic cords.

Clinical connectionTheir organization underlies blood filtration.

16

Trabecular artery

Arterial branch traveling within a splenic connective-tissue trabecula.

What it doesDistributes inflow deeper through parenchyma.

Clinical connectionTraumatic rupture can bleed briskly.

17

Trabecular vein

Venous channel within trabecular connective tissue.

What it doesReturns blood toward splenic vein at hilum.

Clinical connectionDistinct from sinusoidal microvessels.

18

Splenic macrophages

Phagocytes abundant in marginal zone and red pulp.

What it doesClear old erythrocytes and opsonized organisms.

Clinical connectionAsplenia impairs clearance of encapsulated pathogens.

19

Splenic lymphatic outflow

Lymphatic vessels largely associated with capsule and trabeculae.

What it doesDrain tissue fluid toward pancreaticosplenic nodes.

Clinical connectionSplenic blood filtering differs from node lymph filtering.

20

Gastrosplenic ligament

Peritoneal fold between stomach and spleen containing short gastric vessels.

What it doesSuspends spleen and carries vessels to gastric fundus.

Clinical connectionVessels may be injured during splenic mobilization.

Questions people ask

Five questions about Spleen

Short answers grounded in the structures described above. Use the illustrated guide for anatomical context.

What is the spleen's function?

The spleen filters blood, removes aging red blood cells and helps immune cells respond to blood-borne material. Its red and white pulp perform different tasks. Structurally, the periarteriolar lymphoid sheath comprises t-cell-rich white-pulp cylinder surrounding a central arteriole. Physiologically, the periarteriolar lymphoid sheath governs samples blood-borne antigens with nearby immune cells.

Can you feel a normal spleen?

A normal adult spleen is usually protected beneath the left lower ribs and is not readily felt on examination; enlargement is a medical finding, not something this atlas can diagnose. The splenic vein (Vena lienalis) drains the spleen and joins the superior mesenteric vein behind the pancreatic neck to form the portal vein. Physiologically, the splenic vein returns filtered blood to the portal system.

What is the capsule & trabeculae?

A fibroelastic capsule sending trabeculae into the pulp, carrying the vessels. Functionally, it governs structural framework and conduit for vessels. In clinical pathology involving the capsule & trabeculae, the capsule is thin and the pulp fragile — the spleen tears easily in blunt abdominal trauma and bleeds briskly.

Where is the white pulp located?

Periarteriolar lymphoid sheaths (T cells), follicles (B cells) and a marginal zone rich in macrophages. Functionally, it governs immune surveillance of blood: antibody and opsonin production, especially against capsules. Medical evaluation of the white pulp also notes that its loss explains why asplenic patients are vulnerable to pneumococcus, meningococcus and H. influenzae.

What is the function of the hilum?

Functionally, it governs vascular gateway; also the site of accessory spleens in ~15% of people. The notched medial border where splenic vessels enter and the tail of pancreas and stomach fundus relate. Clinically, regarding the hilum, a missed accessory spleen can cause relapse of disease after splenectomy for ITP.
