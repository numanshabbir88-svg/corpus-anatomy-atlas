Overview

How this part of the body works

Shaped like a golf ball resting on a shallow tee, the glenohumeral shoulder joint trades bony depth for unmatched mobility—relying on a muscular cuff to stay centered. Cut-away view of the cuff, labrum, capsule and biceps tendon — the structures behind most shoulder clinics. Within the human body, the shoulder joint (Articulatio humeri) is situated in glenohumeral joint, receives arterial perfusion via anterior & posterior circumflex humeral, and is innervated by axillary nerve.

Anatomically, the supraspinatus tendon (Tendo m. supraspinati) lies over the top of the humeral head. Physiologically, the supraspinatus tendon initiates abduction (first 15°). The infraspinatus tendon (Tendo m. infraspinati) is posterior to the head. Physiologically, the infraspinatus tendon governs external rotation. Anatomically, the teres minor tendon (Tendo m. teretis minoris) lies below infraspinatus. Physiologically, the teres minor tendon governs external rotation + capsule tension. Structurally, the subscapularis tendon (Tendo m. subscapularis) comprises front of the head from the scapular fossa. Physiologically, the subscapularis tendon governs internal rotation; anterior stability. Structurally, the glenoid labrum (Labrum glenoidale) comprises fibrocartilage rim deepening the socket. Physiologically, the glenoid labrum governs adds ~50% of glenoid depth. Structurally, the glenohumeral capsule & ligaments (Capsula articularis) comprises loose sleeve with three glenohumeral ligaments. Physiologically, the glenohumeral capsule & ligaments governs permits huge range; limits extremes.

Clinically, regarding the supraspinatus tendon, most torn cuff tendon — painful arc 60–120°. From a diagnostic standpoint, in the infraspinatus tendon, tear → weak external rotation. In clinical pathology involving the teres minor tendon, part of cuff tears in overhead athletes. Medical evaluation of the subscapularis tendon also notes that lift-off test detects its tear.

Questions people ask

Five questions about Shoulder joint

Short answers grounded in the structures described above. Use the illustrated guide for anatomical context.

Why can the shoulder move so freely?

The shallow glenoid cavity permits a wide range of movement. The labrum, capsule, rotator cuff and surrounding muscles help stabilize the humeral head. Structurally, the glenoid fossa comprises shallow scapular socket deepened by its labrum. Physiologically, the glenoid fossa receives humeral head in shoulder joint.

Which muscles form the rotator cuff?

Supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis form the rotator cuff around the glenohumeral joint. Anatomically, the teres minor tendon (Tendo m. teretis minoris) lies below infraspinatus. Physiologically, the teres minor tendon governs external rotation + capsule tension.

What is the supraspinatus tendon?

Over the top of the humeral head. Functionally, it initiates abduction (first 15°). In clinical pathology involving the supraspinatus tendon, most torn cuff tendon — painful arc 60–120°.

Where is the teres minor tendon located?

Below infraspinatus. Functionally, it governs external rotation + capsule tension. The infraspinatus tendon (Tendo m. infraspinati) is posterior to the head. Medical evaluation of the infraspinatus tendon also notes that tear → weak external rotation.

What is the function of the glenoid labrum?

Functionally, it governs adds ~50% of glenoid depth. Fibrocartilage rim deepening the socket. Clinically, regarding the glenoid labrum, SLAP lesions in throwers; Bankart in dislocation.
