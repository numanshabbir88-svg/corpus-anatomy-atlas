Illustrated structures

Read the anatomy, part by part

These 20 structures describe anatomy related to the chapter’s regional illustration. Each entry separates location, function and a clinical point.

01

Brain

Encephalon

Cerebrum, cerebellum and brainstem inside the cranial vault.

What it doesIntegration, consciousness, motor planning and autonomic control.

Clinical connectionHead injury scales from concussion to diffuse axonal injury.

02

Spinal cord

Medulla spinalis

31-segment cord from foramen magnum to L1–L2.

What it doesTwo-way tracts plus segmental reflex circuits.

Clinical connectionCord transection level predicts deficit pattern precisely.

03

Cervical plexus

Plexus cervicalis (C1–C4)

Nerve network in the posterior triangle of the neck.

What it doesNeck muscles and skin; phrenic nerve to diaphragm.

Clinical connectionC3-4-5 keeps the diaphragm alive — injury above C4 stops breathing.

04

Brachial plexus

Plexus brachialis (C5–T1)

Roots-trunks-divisions-cords-branches behind the clavicle.

What it doesAll motor and sensory supply of the upper limb.

Clinical connectionErb's palsy (C5-6) from birth traction: waiter's-tip hand.

05

Intercostal nerves

Nn. intercostales (T1–T11)

Ventral rami running in subcostal grooves.

What it doesChest-wall muscles and segmental skin band.

Clinical connectionShingles follows one intercostal dermatome.

06

Lumbar plexus

Plexus lumbalis (L1–L4)

Network within psoas major.

What it doesAnterior thigh and medial leg supply.

Clinical connectionFemoral nerve block anaesthetises the anterior thigh.

07

Sacral plexus

Plexus sacralis (L4–S4)

Plexus on the piriformis wall of the pelvis.

What it doesGluteal region, perineum and the sciatic nerve.

Clinical connectionSciatica radiates along its branches down the leg.

08

Sciatic nerve

N. ischiadicus

Largest nerve in the body (L4–S3).

What it doesHamstrings and all muscles below the knee.

Clinical connectionWrong gluteal injection site can injure it — use upper outer quadrant.

09

Sympathetic trunk

Truncus sympathicus

Chain of ganglia from skull base to coccyx.

What it doesFight-or-flight: heart up, gut down, pupils wide.

Clinical connectionHorner's syndrome: ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis.

10

Vagus nerve (CN X)

N. vagus

Longest cranial nerve, neck to pelvis.

What it doesParasympathetic brake on heart, lungs and gut.

Clinical connectionVagal stimulation terminates SVT — the Valsalva trick.

11

Femoral nerve

N. femoralis

L2–L4 branch under the inguinal ligament.

What it doesQuadriceps power and anterior-thigh sensation.

Clinical connectionLoss → buckling knee and absent knee jerk.

12

Dorsal root ganglion

Ganglion sensorium

Sensory cell-body cluster on each dorsal root.

What it doesHouses pseudounipolar sensory neurons.

Clinical connectionVaricella hides here and re-emerges as shingles.

13

Pelvic splanchnic nerves

Parasympathetic fibers from sacral levels S2–S4 travel to pelvic autonomic plexuses.

What it doesReach bladder, rectal and reproductive pelvic organ targets.

Clinical connectionDo not confuse these autonomic fibers with somatic pudendal branches.

14

Inferior hypogastric plexus

Paired networks on the lateral walls of the rectum combine sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers.

What it doesDistribute autonomic pathways to pelvic organs.

Clinical connectionOperations near these plexuses can affect urinary or sexual function.

15

Hypogastric nerves

Descending autonomic connections between the superior and inferior hypogastric plexuses.

What it doesConvey predominantly sympathetic pathways into the pelvis.

Clinical connectionDo not equate them with the somatic pudendal nerve.

16

Pudendal nerve

Paired somatic S2–S4 nerve passes through the pudendal canal toward the perineum.

What it doesSupplies external genital and perineal sensation plus motor branches to sphincters.

Clinical connectionIts terminal genital branch differs between male and female anatomy.

17

Prostatic plexus

Autonomic branches of the inferior hypogastric plexus course along the prostate.

What it doesDistribute fibers to the prostate and continue toward penile erectile tissue.

Clinical connectionNerve-sparing prostate surgery considers this relationship.

18

Cavernous nerves of penis

Small autonomic branches run from the prostatic plexus toward penile erectile tissue.

What it doesRegulate erectile smooth muscle and arterial inflow.

Clinical connectionDifferent from the dorsal penile nerve, which primarily conveys somatic sensation.

19

Dorsal nerve of penis

Terminal pudendal branch runs on the dorsal aspect of the penis toward the glans.

What it doesConveys much of the somatic sensation from the penis and glans.

Clinical connectionDistinguish sensory dorsal nerve from autonomic cavernous nerves.

20

Perineal nerve and posterior scrotal branches

Pudendal perineal branch enters the superficial perineal region and divides into muscular and cutaneous branches.

What it doesSupplies perineal muscles and posterior scrotal skin.

Clinical connectionAnterior scrotal skin also receives ilioinguinal and genitofemoral contributions.

Questions people ask

Five questions about Nervous system — male

Short answers grounded in the structures described above. Use the illustrated guide for anatomical context.

Is the male nervous system built differently from the female nervous system?

The main brain, spinal cord and peripheral nerve plan is shared. A male-focused pelvic view highlights branches serving the prostate, penis and scrotum rather than implying separate male brain or spinal pathways.

Which nerves contribute to penile erection?

Autonomic cavernous nerves arising from the prostatic plexus influence penile smooth muscle and blood flow. Somatic sensation from the penis is carried largely through the dorsal nerve of the penis, a pudendal branch.

What is the pudendal nerve in the male pelvis?

The pudendal nerve derives from sacral segments S2–S4 and supplies much of the perineum. Its branches include the inferior rectal, perineal and dorsal penile nerves; these are distinct from pelvic autonomic nerves.

How do pelvic splanchnic nerves differ from hypogastric nerves?

Pelvic splanchnic nerves carry sacral parasympathetic fibers from S2–S4 into pelvic plexuses. Hypogastric nerves convey predominantly sympathetic pathways from higher plexuses; both contribute to pelvic organ innervation.

Can the full-body male nerve picture show individual cavernous nerves?

No. It is an orientation image for major nervous-system routes. Cavernous and prostatic plexus branches are described as unpinned study structures rather than falsely marked on a whole-body plate.
